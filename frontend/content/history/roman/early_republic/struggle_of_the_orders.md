# The Struggle of the Orders: Rome's Internal Conflict

## Overview

The Struggle of the Orders (494-287 BCE) was the prolonged conflict between patricians and plebeians that shaped the Roman Republic's political development. Through a combination of secessions, legislation, and gradual reform, the plebeians won access to magistracies, priesthoods, and legal protections, creating the mixed constitution that defined the classical Republic.

## The Initial Crisis

### Plebeian Grievances

At the Republic's founding, plebeians faced severe disadvantages:
- **Debt Bondage**: Defaulting debtors could be enslaved by creditors
- **Political Exclusion**: Barred from consulship and priesthoods
- **Legal Ignorance**: Laws were unwritten, interpreted by patrician priests
- **Economic Distress**: Small farmers suffered from constant warfare

### The First Secession (494 BCE)

The plebeians took dramatic action:
- Withdrew to the Sacred Mount (*Mons Sacer*) outside Rome
- Threatened to found a separate city
- Rome's army—largely plebeian—was effectively on strike

### The Tribunate of the Plebs

Negotiations produced revolutionary concessions:
- **Tribunes of the Plebs**: Officers elected by plebeians to protect them
- **Sacrosanctity**: Tribunes were inviolable; harming them was a religious crime
- **Intercessio**: Tribunes could veto any magistrate's action
- **Concilium Plebis**: Assembly of plebeians alone

## Key Developments

### The Twelve Tables (451-450 BCE)

Plebeian demands for written laws led to:
- Commission of ten men (*decemviri*) to codify laws
- Twelve bronze tablets displayed in the Forum
- First written Roman law code
- Though harsh, laws were now public and predictable

Cicero later wrote that schoolboys memorized the Twelve Tables "like a necessary hymn."

### The Valerio-Horatian Laws (449 BCE)

After abuse of power by the decemvirs:
- Restored tribunes and right of appeal (*provocatio*)
- Declared plebiscites binding on the whole state (disputed by patricians)
- Confirmed tribune sacrosanctity

### The Canuleian Law (445 BCE)

Removed prohibition on patrician-plebeian marriage (*conubium*), allowing social integration of the orders.

### Military Tribunes with Consular Power (444-367 BCE)

A compromise position:
- *Tribuni militum consulari potestate*: Six military tribunes could be elected instead of consuls
- Open to plebeians (though patricians usually won)
- Allowed gradual plebeian advancement

### The Licinian-Sextian Rogations (367 BCE)

Major breakthrough after another secession:
- One consul must be plebeian
- Limits on land occupation from *ager publicus*
- Debt relief provisions
- Created the praetorship (for patricians)

Gaius Licinius Stolo and Lucius Sextius Lateranus, tribunes, pushed these through after ten years of struggle.

### Opening Religious Offices

- **Lex Ogulnia (300 BCE)**: Plebeians admitted to pontificate and augural colleges
- Broke the patrician monopoly on religious knowledge

### The Hortensian Law (287 BCE)

The final victory:
- Plebiscites (*plebiscita*) declared binding on all citizens without senatorial approval
- Concilium Plebis could legislate for the entire state
- Marked the traditional end of the Struggle

## Outcomes and Significance

### The New Nobility

Rather than ending aristocracy, the Struggle created a new elite:
- **Nobiles**: Families (patrician or plebeian) with consular ancestors
- **Novi Homines**: "New men" without noble ancestry (rare consuls)
- Political oligarchy maintained through wealth and prestige

### Constitutional Balance

The Roman constitution balanced competing interests:
- **Consuls**: Executive power, military command
- **Senate**: Advisory body of ex-magistrates; controlled finance and foreign policy
- **Assemblies**: Legislative and electoral functions
- **Tribunes**: Protection against magisterial abuse

This "mixed constitution" was praised by Polybius as combining monarchical, aristocratic, and democratic elements.

### Legal Development

The Struggle produced lasting legal innovations:
- Written law code
- Right of appeal
- Protection against arbitrary power
- Foundation for Roman legal tradition

## Primary Sources and Evidence

Most accounts come from later authors (Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus) writing centuries after events. Modern scholars debate:
- How much detail is historical vs. legendary
- Whether the conflict was as sharp as portrayed
- The nature of early plebeian organization

### Livy's Account

Livy (*Ab Urbe Condita*, Books 2-10) provides the fullest narrative, though influenced by:
- Later political conflicts (especially the Gracchi)
- Patriotic idealization
- Limited sources for early period

# The Five Good Emperors: Rome's Golden Age

## Overview

The period from 96 to 180 CE is often called the age of the "Five Good Emperors"—Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius. These emperors, chosen by adoption rather than birth, presided over what Edward Gibbon called "the period in the history of the world during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous." Under their rule, the Roman Empire reached its greatest territorial extent and enjoyed remarkable stability.

## The Adoptive Principle

### Breaking the Hereditary Pattern

Unlike the Julio-Claudians and Flavians, these emperors adopted their successors based on merit:
- Nerva adopted Trajan
- Trajan adopted Hadrian
- Hadrian adopted Antoninus Pius (who adopted Marcus Aurelius)
- Marcus Aurelius broke the pattern by leaving power to his biological son, Commodus

This "luck" was partly due to the first four lacking biological sons.

### The Ideal of the Optimus Princeps

The emperors were seen as the "best ruler" (*optimus princeps*):
- First among equals, not absolute monarchs
- Ruling for the benefit of subjects
- Respectful of Senate and law
- Personally virtuous

## The Five Emperors

### Nerva (96-98 CE)

An elderly senator chosen after Domitian's assassination:
- Ended Domitian's tyranny and persecutions
- Recalled exiles; returned confiscated property
- Faced military discontent; adopted Trajan to secure army loyalty
- Brief reign (16 months) but crucial transition

### Trajan (98-117 CE)

The first emperor from outside Italy (Spain), Trajan was a soldier's emperor:

**Military Achievements**:
- **Dacian Wars (101-106 CE)**: Conquered Dacia (modern Romania); Trajan's Column commemorates campaign
- **Parthian War (114-117 CE)**: Conquered Armenia and Mesopotamia; empire at greatest extent
- Earned title *Optimus* ("The Best")

**Domestic Policy**:
- Extensive building program (Trajan's Forum, Trajan's Market)
- *Alimenta*: Support for poor children in Italy
- Good relations with Senate
- Correspondence with Pliny the Younger reveals humane governance

**Character**:
Cassius Dio wrote that he was "most conspicuous for his justice, for his bravery, and for the simplicity of his habits."

### Hadrian (117-138 CE)

Trajan's ward and successor, Hadrian was a complex, cultured figure:

**Policy of Consolidation**:
- Abandoned Trajan's eastern conquests as unsustainable
- Built defensive frontiers:
  - **Hadrian's Wall** (Britain): 73 miles across northern England
  - German-Raetian Limes
- Traveled extensively throughout empire (12+ years away from Rome)

**Cultural Interests**:
- Passionate Hellenophile ("Graeculus")
- Wrote poetry in Greek and Latin
- Rebuilt the Pantheon (with its perfect dome)
- Villa at Tivoli recreated sites from his travels
- Relationship with Antinous (who was deified after drowning)

**Relations with Senate**:
More difficult than predecessors; executed four consulars at reign's start and end.

**Jewish Revolt (132-136 CE)**:
Bar Kokhba revolt devastated Judaea; Jerusalem refounded as Aelia Capitolina.

### Antoninus Pius (138-161 CE)

The most peaceful reign; he never left Italy:

**Characteristics**:
- Gentle, fair administration
- Avoided unnecessary wars
- Extended Roman citizenship
- Good relations with Senate
- Financially prudent (left large surplus)

**Cognomen "Pius"**:
- Possibly for devotion to adoptive father Hadrian
- Or for general piety and duty

**Longevity**:
23-year reign; one of the longest and least eventful—which was the achievement.

### Marcus Aurelius (161-180 CE)

The "philosopher-emperor"; co-ruled initially with Lucius Verus (d. 169 CE):

**Military Challenges**:
Unlike predecessors, Marcus faced constant warfare:
- **Parthian War (161-166 CE)**: Victory but troops brought plague
- **Marcomannic Wars (166-180 CE)**: Germanic tribes invaded; Marcus spent most of reign on Danube frontier
- Died at Vindobona (Vienna) during campaign

**The Meditations**:
Marcus's private philosophical diary, written in Greek during campaigns:
> "Begin the morning by saying to yourself: I shall meet with the meddlesome, the ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly."

Stoic reflections on duty, mortality, and virtue; one of antiquity's most personal documents.

**Persecution of Christians**:
Ironically, the philosopher-emperor's reign saw significant Christian persecution (e.g., martyrs of Lyon, 177 CE).

**The Fatal Choice**:
Marcus's son **Commodus** was biological heir. Breaking the adoptive pattern, Marcus left power to him—beginning the empire's decline.

## Achievements of the Era

### Government

- Efficient provincial administration
- Expansion of citizenship
- Humane treatment of slaves (legal protections)
- Development of Roman law

### Economy

- Peak of Mediterranean trade
- Urban prosperity
- Standardized coinage
- Infrastructure maintenance

### Culture

- Flowering of Greek literature (Second Sophistic)
- Philosophical schools thrived
- Monumental architecture
- Art combining Greek and Roman traditions

## The End of the Golden Age

### Commodus (180-192 CE)

Marcus's choice proved disastrous:
- Ended the frontier wars by concessions
- Identified himself with Hercules
- Increasingly erratic and cruel
- Assassinated on December 31, 192 CE

### Gibbon's Assessment

Edward Gibbon famously wrote:
> "If a man were called to fix the period in the history of the world during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous, he would, without hesitation, name that which elapsed from the death of Domitian to the accession of Commodus."

This romantic view has been qualified by modern scholars who note:
- Slavery remained fundamental
- Frontier wars were constant
- The Antonine Plague (165-180 CE) killed millions
- Provincial populations had limited political voice

Yet the era's relative stability and prosperity remain remarkable.

# The Pax Romana: Rome at Its Height

## Overview

The Pax Romana ("Roman Peace") describes approximately two centuries of relative peace, stability, and prosperity across the Roman Empire, traditionally dated from Augustus's reign (27 BCE) to the death of Marcus Aurelius (180 CE). During this era, the empire reached its greatest extent, trade flourished across three continents, and a common culture united diverse peoples under Roman rule.

## Defining the Pax Romana

### What "Peace" Meant

The Pax Romana was not absolute peace:
- Frontier wars continued (Britain, Dacia, Parthia)
- Civil conflicts occurred (Year of Four Emperors, 69 CE)
- Revolts were suppressed (Judaea, 66-73 CE; Bar Kokhba, 132-136 CE)

Rather, it meant:
- End of large-scale civil wars that had devastated the late Republic
- Stability of the political system
- Safety of travel and trade
- Consistent administration

### Extent of the Empire

At its height under Trajan (117 CE), the empire encompassed:
- Total area: approximately 5 million square kilometers
- Population: 55-70 million people
- From Britain to Mesopotamia
- From the Rhine and Danube to the Sahara

## Government and Administration

### The Emperor

The emperor (*imperator*, *princeps*, *Augustus*) was:
- Supreme military commander
- Chief priest (*pontifex maximus*)
- Source of law and justice
- Divine or semi-divine figure

Imperial power was theoretically not hereditary but in practice passed within dynasties.

### Provincial Administration

**Types of Provinces**:
- Imperial provinces: Governed by emperor's legates
- Senatorial provinces: Governed by proconsuls
- Equestrian provinces: Egypt and small provinces

**Provincial Government**:
- Governors collected taxes, administered justice, maintained order
- Cities had significant local autonomy
- Greek East retained its language and institutions

### The Role of Cities

The empire was a network of cities:
- Over 2,000 cities by the 2nd century
- Local elites (*curiales*) administered their own affairs
- Competition for status through buildings, festivals, imperial honors
- Romanization through urban culture

## Economy

### Trade Networks

The Pax Romana facilitated unprecedented commerce:
- Mediterranean as "Roman lake"
- Roads connected all provinces (50,000 miles of paved roads)
- Common coinage, weights, and measures
- Reduction of piracy and banditry

**Trade Routes**:
- Silk Road brought Chinese silk and spices
- Indian Ocean trade via Red Sea ports
- Atlantic routes to Britain
- Trans-Saharan gold trade

### Agriculture

Most people were farmers:
- Italy: vines, olives, grain
- Egypt: grain basket of the empire
- North Africa: grain, olive oil
- Gaul: wine, manufactured goods

**Villa Economy**: Large estates (*latifundia*) worked by slaves or tenant farmers (*coloni*)

### Manufacturing

Important industries included:
- Pottery (Terra Sigillata from Gaul)
- Textiles (Eastern Mediterranean)
- Metalwork (Spain, Britain)
- Glass (Syria, Egypt)

## Society

### Social Hierarchy

**Citizens**:
- Senators: Highest rank; minimum 1 million sesterces
- Equestrians: Wealthy businessmen and administrators
- Plebeians: Common citizens
- Freedmen: Former slaves with limited rights

**Non-Citizens**:
- Peregrini: Free non-citizens; majority of population
- Slaves: Property with no rights
- 212 CE: Caracalla's *Constitutio Antoniniana* granted citizenship to all free inhabitants

### Urban Life

Cities featured:
- Forums: Public squares for business and politics
- Temples: Traditional and imperial cults
- Baths: Social centers; free or inexpensive
- Amphitheaters: Gladiatorial games
- Theaters: Drama and entertainment
- Aqueducts: Clean water supply

### Religion

Religious diversity flourished:
- Traditional Roman gods
- Emperor worship (especially in East)
- Mystery religions: Isis, Mithras, Cybele
- Judaism: Protected but separate
- Christianity: Gradually spreading; periodic persecution

## Culture and Learning

### Literature

Major authors of the early empire:
- **Seneca** (4 BCE-65 CE): Stoic philosophy, tragedies
- **Petronius** (d. 66 CE): *Satyricon*
- **Pliny the Elder** (23-79 CE): *Natural History*
- **Tacitus** (56-120 CE): *Annals*, *Histories*, *Germania*
- **Juvenal** (55-130 CE): Satires
- **Plutarch** (46-120 CE): *Parallel Lives*
- **Suetonius** (69-122 CE): *Lives of the Caesars*
- **Apuleius** (124-170 CE): *The Golden Ass*

### Philosophy

Stoicism dominated among the elite:
- **Seneca**: Advisor to Nero
- **Epictetus**: Former slave; taught practical ethics
- **Marcus Aurelius**: Emperor-philosopher

### Architecture

Distinctive Roman achievements:
- **Concrete construction**: Enabled domes and vaults
- **Pantheon** (126 CE): Perfect dome; still standing
- **Colosseum** (80 CE): 50,000 capacity
- **Trajan's Column**: Narrative sculpture
- Provincial cities: Temples, baths, theaters replicated throughout empire

### Law

Roman law became increasingly sophisticated:
- Jurists: Gaius, Papinian, Ulpian, Paul
- Citizenship expanded legal rights
- Foundation of European legal tradition
- Culminated in Justinian's *Corpus Juris Civilis* (6th century CE)

## The Limits of Peace

### Frontier Defense

The army maintained the frontiers:
- 25-30 legions (150,000-180,000 legionaries)
- Equal numbers of auxiliary troops
- Permanent camps became towns
- Linear defenses: Hadrian's Wall, German Limes

### Internal Tensions

The peace masked ongoing issues:
- Inequality between rich and poor
- Slave labor and slave revolts
- Provincial resentment of taxation
- Succession crises

### The Antonine Plague (165-180 CE)

Devastating epidemic (possibly smallpox):
- Brought from the East by returning armies
- Killed millions (10-18 million estimated)
- Weakened military and economy
- Marked beginning of troubles to come

## End of the Pax Romana

Marcus Aurelius's death (180 CE) traditionally marks the end:
- Commodus (180-192 CE): Erratic rule
- Year of Five Emperors (193 CE)
- Severan Dynasty: Militarization of government
- Third Century Crisis: Near-collapse of empire

## Key Dates

# Romulus: King, God, or Invention?

## Introduction

The story of Romulus, the legendary founder of Rome, occupies a unique place in ancient Roman history, serving as a bridge between mythology and the nascent reality of Roman civilization. According to tradition, Romulus, alongside his twin brother Remus, established Rome in 753 BCE, marking the beginning of the Regal Period. His tale, however, is enshrined in a mixture of myth, legend, and historical conjecture, which has sparked ongoing debates among historians about his existence and role. This essay examines Romulus as a historical figure, a deity, and a potential literary invention, drawing on primary literary, archaeological, and epigraphic sources, as well as modern scholarly interpretations. By exploring these facets, we aim to ascertain whether Romulus was indeed a king, a god, or merely an invention of later Roman imagination.

## The Mythical Origins of Romulus

Romulus's narrative begins with a myth steeped in divine intervention and fratricide. According to Livy ("Ab Urbe Condita" 1.3-7) and Plutarch ("Life of Romulus" 3-4), Romulus and Remus were born to Rhea Silvia, a Vestal Virgin, and Mars, the god of war. Their early life was tumultuous, as they were abandoned by the Tiber River and subsequently nurtured by a she-wolf (lupa). This origin story serves not only as a mythical foundation for Rome's greatness but also as an allegory of the city's resilience and divine favor.

### Student Note: Key Takeaways
- **Romulus and Remus**: Legendary twin founders of Rome.
- **Lupa**: Latin for "she-wolf"; symbolic of Rome's resilience.

### General Reader Context
The tale of Romulus and Remus encapsulates the Roman spirit of overcoming adversity, a narrative that would resonate throughout Rome's imperial history.

## Historiographical Debates: King, God, or Invention?

The figure of Romulus raises significant historiographical questions. Was he a real historical figure, a deified hero, or an invention of later Roman writers? Scholars such as T.P. Wiseman have argued that Romulus was a later literary creation designed to provide Rome with a noble origin story. Conversely, others, including Andrea Carandini, suggest archaeological evidence supports the existence of a proto-Roman settlement dating back to the 8th century BCE, potentially validating Romulus's historicity.

### Professor's Sidebar: Scholarly Debates
- **Wiseman, T.P.**: Argues Romulus is a literary invention.
- **Carandini, Andrea**: Supports Romulus's historicity through archaeology.

### Historian's Methodology
Assessing Romulus involves cross-referencing literary texts like those of Livy and Plutarch with archaeological findings from sites like the Palatine Hill.

## Archaeological Evidence and Material Culture

The archaeological record provides intriguing, albeit inconclusive, evidence for the existence of an early settlement in Rome. Excavations on the Palatine Hill have revealed post holes suggesting a primitive settlement circa 750 BCE, aligning with the traditional date of Rome's foundation (Carandini, "Rome: Day One"). These findings imply the presence of a settlement that could have been the nucleus of a nascent city-state. However, the lack of direct evidence for Romulus himself leaves room for interpretation.

- **Palatine Hill**: One of the Seven Hills of Rome, traditionally the site of Rome's founding.

### Timeline Connection
- **753 BCE**: Traditional date of Rome's founding.

## Literary Sources and Critical Evaluation

Primary sources such as Livy's "Ab Urbe Condita" and Plutarch's "Life of Romulus" offer detailed accounts of Romulus's actions and legacy. However, these texts were written centuries after the supposed events, which necessitates a cautious approach to their historical accuracy. Livy's account, for example, is often seen as an attempt to create a coherent narrative of Rome's origins, blending fact and fiction to instill pride and a sense of unity among Roman citizens.

### General Reader Context
The blending of myth and history in these accounts reflects a broader ancient tradition of using origin stories to reinforce cultural identity.

## Epigraphic Evidence and Interpretations

Epigraphic sources, although sparse for the Regal Period, provide some insights into the early Roman political and religious landscape. Inscriptions and remnants of early Latin script hint at the development of a structured society with religious practices that may have included veneration of founder figures like Romulus.

## Romulus as a Deity and Cultural Symbol

Romulus's transformation from a legendary king to a god, Quirinus, reflects the Roman practice of deifying esteemed leaders. This elevation to divinity served to legitimize Rome's origins and its ruling class. The cult of Quirinus, as noted in various inscriptions (CIL), exemplifies the intertwining of politics and religion in Roman society.

### Student Note: Vocabulary
- **Quirinus**: Deified form of Romulus, associated with Roman statehood.

### Map Reference
- **Rome**: Center of the Roman world and the focal point of the narrative around Romulus.

## Conclusion

The figure of Romulus remains a complex tapestry woven from myth, potential historical reality, and later literary embellishment. While archaeological and epigraphic evidence suggests the existence of an early settlement that could align with the traditional narrative, the absence of definitive proof of Romulus's historicity keeps the debate alive. Romulus, as both a king and a god, embodies the Roman ideals of resilience, divine favor, and imperial destiny. His story—whether fact or fiction—continues to captivate scholars and laypeople alike, serving as a testament to the enduring power of myth in shaping historical consciousness.

### Broader Significance
The enduring myth of Romulus exemplifies the power of narrative in constructing national identity and cultural heritage. Whether as a historical figure or a symbolic invention, Romulus remains integral to understanding Rome's foundational ethos, reflecting broader themes of leadership, divinity, and legacy.

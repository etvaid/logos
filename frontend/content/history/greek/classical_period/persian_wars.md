# The Persian Wars: Greece's Fight for Survival

## Overview

The Persian Wars (499-449 BCE) represent one of history's most consequential conflicts. When the vast Achaemenid Persian Empire, the greatest power the ancient world had seen, launched invasions of the Greek mainland, the fractious Greek poleis united to defend their freedom. The Greek victories at Marathon, Thermopylae, Salamis, and Plataea not only preserved Greek independence but shaped the trajectory of Western civilization.

## The Achaemenid Empire

### Imperial Background

Founded by Cyrus the Great (r. 559-530 BCE), the Persian Empire stretched from Egypt to Central Asia, encompassing approximately 50 million people—nearly half the world's population. Key rulers:

- **Cyrus II "the Great"** (559-530 BCE): Conquered Media, Lydia, and Babylon
- **Cambyses II** (530-522 BCE): Conquered Egypt
- **Darius I "the Great"** (522-486 BCE): Expanded into Thrace and organized the empire
- **Xerxes I** (486-465 BCE): Led the great invasion of Greece

### The Ionian Greeks

Greek cities on the Aegean coast of Asia Minor (Ionia) had been under Persian rule since Cyrus's conquest of Lydia (546 BCE). Their status as Persian subjects set the stage for conflict.

## The Ionian Revolt (499-494 BCE)

### The Spark

In 499 BCE, the tyrant Aristagoras of Miletus, facing Persian displeasure, led Ionia in revolt. Athens and Eretria sent limited support—20 and 5 ships respectively. The rebels briefly captured and burned Sardis, the Persian regional capital.

### Persian Response

Darius crushed the revolt by 494 BCE. Miletus was destroyed, its population enslaved or deported. Herodotus reports that Darius, upon learning of Athens's involvement:

> "Master, remember the Athenians."

This was reportedly announced to him at every dinner thereafter.

## The First Persian Invasion (492-490 BCE)

### Mardonius's Expedition (492 BCE)

Darius sent his son-in-law Mardonius with a fleet and army. Though Thrace and Macedonia submitted, the fleet was wrecked off Mount Athos and the expedition abandoned.

### Marathon (490 BCE)

**The Campaign**
Darius dispatched a punitive expedition under Datis and Artaphernes directly across the Aegean. After destroying Eretria, the Persians landed at Marathon, northeast of Athens.

**The Battle**
The Athenian assembly debated response. Miltiades convinced the polemarch Callimachus to attack. The Athenian hoplites, with Plataean allies, numbered about 10,000 against perhaps 25,000 Persians.

Miltiades weakened his center and strengthened his wings. The Persian center broke through but was then enveloped. According to Herodotus, 6,400 Persians fell against only 192 Athenians.

**The Legacy**
The runner Pheidippides (or Philippides) allegedly ran to Athens with news of victory—the origin of the modern marathon race. The Athenian dead were buried on the battlefield, where their tomb mound stands today.

## The Inter-War Period (490-480 BCE)

### Themistocles and Athenian Naval Power

The Athenian statesman Themistocles convinced Athens to use newly discovered silver from Laurium to build a fleet of 200 triremes. This decision proved decisive.

### Persian Preparations

Darius died in 486 BCE. His son Xerxes spent years preparing the greatest military expedition the ancient world had seen:
- A canal across the Athos peninsula
- Double pontoon bridges across the Hellespont
- Supply depots along the route
- An army of approximately 100,000-300,000 (ancient sources wildly exaggerate)

## The Second Persian Invasion (480-479 BCE)

### Thermopylae and Artemisium (480 BCE)

**The Strategy**
The Greeks chose to make their stand at the narrow pass of Thermopylae while the fleet held the straits of Artemisium.

**Leonidas and the Three Hundred**
King Leonidas I of Sparta commanded the pass with 300 Spartan warriors, 700 Thespians, and other Greek allies. When betrayed by Ephialtes, who revealed a mountain path, Leonidas dismissed most allies but stayed with his Spartans and the Thespians.

The epitaph composed by Simonides became immortal:

> Ὦ ξεῖν', ἀγγέλλειν Λακεδαιμονίοις ὅτι τῇδε
> κείμεθα, τοῖς κείνων ῥήμασι πειθόμενοι.

> "Stranger, go tell the Spartans that here
> we lie, obedient to their commands."

### The Sack of Athens

With Thermopylae lost, central Greece lay open. Athens was evacuated; the Persians burned the Acropolis.

### Salamis (September 480 BCE)

**Themistocles's Stratagem**
The Greek fleet gathered at Salamis. When the Peloponnesians wanted to retreat to the Isthmus, Themistocles sent a secret message to Xerxes claiming he would betray the Greeks—inducing the Persians to attack in the confined waters.

**The Battle**
In the narrow straits, Persian numerical superiority became a disadvantage. The Greeks, with superior seamanship and heavier ships, destroyed about 200 Persian vessels while losing only 40. Xerxes, watching from his throne on Mount Aigaleos, returned to Asia.

### Plataea (479 BCE)

The Persian general Mardonius remained with a large army. At Plataea in Boeotia, a Greek force under the Spartan regent Pausanias won a decisive victory. On the same day, tradition held, the Greek fleet destroyed the remnants of the Persian navy at Mycale.

## Aftermath and Legacy

### The Delian League

Athens organized an alliance of Aegean states (478 BCE), ostensibly to continue war against Persia. The League's treasury, initially on Delos, was moved to Athens (454 BCE), transforming alliance into empire.

### Cultural Impact

Victory generated immense Greek pride and a sense of cultural superiority over "barbarians." This confidence fueled the artistic and intellectual achievements of the Classical period.

Aeschylus's tragedy *The Persians* (472 BCE), our earliest surviving Greek drama, depicts the Persian court receiving news of Salamis—remarkable for its sympathetic portrayal of the enemy.

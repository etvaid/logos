# Classical Greek Drama: Tragedy and Comedy

## Overview

Greek drama emerged in Athens during the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, developing from religious rituals into sophisticated art forms that explored fundamental questions of human existence. The tragedians Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, along with the comedian Aristophanes, created works that remain central to world literature and continue to be performed today.

## Origins of Drama

### Religious Roots

Greek drama originated in the worship of Dionysus, god of wine and fertility:
- **Dithyramb**: Choral song to Dionysus, origin of tragedy
- **Thespis** (c. 534 BCE): Traditionally credited with inventing the actor
- **Phallic Songs**: Origin of comedy, according to Aristotle

### The City Dionysia

The major dramatic festival, held annually in late March:
- Three days of tragedy (three playwrights, each presenting three tragedies + one satyr play)
- One day of comedy (five playwrights, each presenting one comedy)
- Competition judged by selected citizens
- The entire city attended; prisoners were released temporarily

### Theater Architecture

The Theater of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis:
- **Orchestra**: Circular dancing floor for the chorus
- **Theatron**: Seating area carved into the hillside (15,000+ capacity)
- **Skene**: Stage building serving as backdrop and dressing room
- **Mechane**: Crane for "flying" characters (deus ex machina)
- **Ekkyklema**: Wheeled platform to reveal interior scenes

## Tragedy

### Characteristics

- **Hamartia**: Error or flaw leading to the hero's downfall
- **Peripeteia**: Reversal of fortune
- **Anagnorisis**: Recognition or discovery
- **Catharsis**: Emotional purification through pity and fear (Aristotle)

### Aeschylus (c. 525-456 BCE)

The first great tragedian, Aeschylus fought at Marathon and Salamis. He introduced the second actor, allowing for dialogue between characters.

**Major Works**:
- ***The Persians*** (472 BCE): Only surviving tragedy on a historical subject; depicts the Persian court learning of Salamis
- ***Seven Against Thebes*** (467 BCE): Theban saga
- ***Oresteia*** (458 BCE): The only complete trilogy surviving
  - *Agamemnon*: The king's murder by his wife Clytemnestra
  - *Libation Bearers*: Orestes's revenge
  - *Eumenides*: Orestes's trial and acquittal in Athens

**Themes**: Divine justice, hereditary curse, the evolution of civilization from vendetta to law.

### Sophocles (c. 496-406 BCE)

Sophocles introduced the third actor and scene-painting. He won first prize over twenty times.

**Major Works**:
- ***Ajax*** (c. 450 BCE): The hero's madness and suicide
- ***Antigone*** (c. 442 BCE): Conflict between divine and human law
- ***Oedipus the King*** (c. 429 BCE): Aristotle's model tragedy
- ***Electra*** (c. 410 BCE): Orestes's revenge through Electra's eyes
- ***Oedipus at Colonus*** (401 BCE, posthumous): The hero's death and transfiguration

**The Oedipus Myth**: Oedipus, unaware of his origins, kills his father and marries his mother, fulfilling the prophecy he tried to escape.

**Themes**: Human nobility in suffering, the limits of human knowledge, divine inscrutability.

> "Call no man happy until he is dead." (Oedipus at Colonus)

### Euripides (c. 484-406 BCE)

The youngest and most controversial tragedian, Euripides won only five first prizes but became the most popular after his death.

**Major Works**:
- ***Medea*** (431 BCE): A woman's terrible revenge on her faithless husband
- ***Hippolytus*** (428 BCE): Destructive passion and divine cruelty
- ***Trojan Women*** (415 BCE): War's devastation of the innocent
- ***Electra*** (c. 413 BCE): Matricide presented unsparingly
- ***Bacchae*** (405 BCE, posthumous): Dionysus's terrible vengeance

**Characteristics**:
- Psychological realism
- Sympathy for women and the marginalized
- Skepticism toward traditional religion
- Innovative use of prologue and deus ex machina

Aristophanes mocked him; Aristotle called him "the most tragic of the poets."

## Comedy

### Old Comedy

**Characteristics**:
- Direct political satire and personal attacks
- Fantasy and absurdity
- Chorus playing central role
- Parabasis: Chorus breaks the fourth wall to address the audience

### Aristophanes (c. 446-386 BCE)

Eleven comedies survive from over forty written:

**Major Works**:
- ***Acharnians*** (425 BCE): Individual peace with Sparta
- ***Knights*** (424 BCE): Attack on the demagogue Cleon
- ***Clouds*** (423 BCE): Satire of Socrates and new education
- ***Wasps*** (422 BCE): Athenian addiction to litigation
- ***Peace*** (421 BCE): Celebrating the Peace of Nicias
- ***Birds*** (414 BCE): Fantasy city in the sky
- ***Lysistrata*** (411 BCE): Women's sex strike to end war
- ***Frogs*** (405 BCE): Dionysus goes to Hades to bring back Euripides

**Themes**: Critique of war, nostalgia for traditional values, suspicion of intellectuals and demagogues.

### New Comedy (later fourth century)

After Athens's political decline, comedy became less political and more focused on domestic situations:
- **Menander** (c. 342-291 BCE): Only *Dyskolos* survives complete
- Stock characters: clever slave, young lovers, stern father
- Influenced Roman comedy (Plautus, Terence) and through them, all later Western comedy

## The Role of the Chorus

The chorus was central to Greek drama:
- **Tragedy**: 12-15 members; comments on action, provides moral perspective
- **Comedy**: 24 members; often in fanciful costumes (birds, wasps, clouds)
- Sang and danced to musical accompaniment
- Gradually diminished in importance through the fifth century

## Performance Conventions

- **Masks**: All performers wore masks; enabled few actors to play many roles
- **Costumes**: Tragic actors wore elevated boots (*kothornoi*); comic actors had padded costumes
- **All-Male Casts**: Female roles played by men
- **Music**: Substantial musical element, now lost
- **Movement**: Stylized, visible from great distances

## Aristotle's *Poetics*

The first work of literary theory, analyzing tragedy:
- **Plot** (*mythos*): Most important element
- **Character** (*ethos*): Should be appropriate, consistent
- **Thought** (*dianoia*): Intellectual content
- **Diction** (*lexis*): Use of language
- **Song** (*melos*): Musical element
- **Spectacle** (*opsis*): Visual elements

The *Poetics*' influence on Western drama and literary theory is immeasurable.

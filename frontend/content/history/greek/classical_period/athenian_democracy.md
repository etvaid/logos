# Athenian Democracy: The Birth of Popular Government

## Overview

Athenian democracy (*demokratia*—"rule of the people") stands as history's first experiment in direct popular government. For nearly two centuries (508-322 BCE), the citizens of Athens governed themselves through institutions that allowed ordinary men to participate in political decision-making. Though limited by modern standards—excluding women, slaves, and foreigners—Athenian democracy remains a foundational reference point for political thought.

## Origins and Development

### Aristocratic Background

Before democracy, Athens was governed by aristocrats (*eupatridai*, "well-born"). Key offices included:
- **Archons**: Nine annually selected magistrates
- **Areopagus**: Council of ex-archons with judicial and supervisory powers
- **Eupatrid families**: Controlled land, religion, and law

### Solon's Reforms (594 BCE)

Appointed as mediator during economic crisis, Solon implemented foundational changes:

**Economic Reforms**:
- Cancelled debts (*seisachtheia*, "shaking off of burdens")
- Prohibited debt slavery
- Reformed weights, measures, and coinage

**Political Reforms**:
- Divided citizens into four property classes
- Opened political offices based on wealth, not birth
- Created the Council of Four Hundred (*boule*)
- Established the *heliaia* (people's court)

Solon famously refused to become tyrant and left Athens, saying:
> "I gave the common people such privilege as is sufficient for them."

### Cleisthenes and the Democratic Revolution (508/7 BCE)

Following the expulsion of the Peisistratid tyrants, Cleisthenes implemented radical reforms that created democracy:

**Reorganization of the Citizen Body**:
- Replaced four traditional tribes with ten new ones
- Each tribe combined three *trittyes* (thirds): one from city, coast, and inland
- The basic unit became the *deme* (village/neighborhood), of which there were 139
- Identity now: "Pericles, son of Xanthippus, of the deme Cholargos"

**New Institutions**:
- **Council of Five Hundred** (*boule*): 50 members from each tribe, chosen by lot
- **Assembly** (*ekklesia*): Gained increased powers
- **Ostracism**: Citizens could vote to exile anyone threatening democracy for ten years

## Democratic Institutions

### The Assembly (Ekklesia)

The sovereign body of Athenian democracy:
- Met 40+ times per year on the Pnyx hill
- Any male citizen could speak and vote
- Quorum of 6,000 for certain decisions
- Decided war and peace, treaties, laws, and major policies
- Voting by show of hands (*cheirotonia*) for most matters

### The Council of Five Hundred (Boule)

Prepared business for the Assembly:
- 500 members chosen by lot, 50 from each tribe
- Could serve twice in a lifetime (not consecutively)
- The *prytaneis* (50 tribal representatives) served as executive committee for 1/10 of the year
- One *epistates* (chairman) chosen daily by lot—for 24 hours, he held the state seal

### The Law Courts (Dikasteria)

Popular justice was central to democracy:
- Large juries (201-6,001 members) chosen by lot
- No professional judges or lawyers
- Citizens prosecuted and defended themselves
- Payment for jury service (*misthos*) enabled poor citizens to participate

### Magistracies

Most officials were selected by lot (*sortition*):
- Annual terms, subject to scrutiny (*dokimasia*) before and accounting (*euthyna*) after
- Military offices (strategoi) elected—the exception proving the rule
- Ten *strategoi* (generals), one per tribe, were the most powerful officials

## The Radical Democracy (462-322 BCE)

### Ephialtes's Reforms (462 BCE)

Ephialtes stripped remaining powers from the aristocratic Areopagus:
- Judicial and supervisory powers transferred to democratic bodies
- The Areopagus retained only jurisdiction over homicide

### The Age of Pericles (c. 460-429 BCE)

Pericles dominated Athens for thirty years through annual election as strategos:
- Introduced pay for jurors and council members
- Citizenship law (451 BCE): Both parents must be Athenian citizens
- Built the Parthenon and other Acropolis monuments
- Led Athens during the early Peloponnesian War

His Funeral Oration (431 BCE), as reported by Thucydides, articulates democratic ideals:

> "Our constitution is called a democracy because power is in the hands not of a minority but of the whole people. When it is a question of settling private disputes, everyone is equal before the law... What counts is not membership of a particular class, but the actual ability which the man possesses."

## Participation and Exclusion

### Who Participated

Adult male citizens (*politai*) over 18 years (for Assembly; 30 for most offices):
- Approximately 30,000-60,000 adult male citizens
- Typical Assembly attendance: 6,000-8,000
- Active participation required leisure—critics argued this favored the wealthy

### Who Was Excluded

The majority of the population:
- **Women**: No political rights; legally represented by male guardians (*kyrios*)
- **Metics** (*metoikoi*): Resident foreigners; paid taxes and served in military but couldn't own land or vote
- **Slaves**: Perhaps 80,000-100,000; no rights whatsoever

## Criticisms Ancient and Modern

### Ancient Critics

Plato, in the *Republic*, argued democracy was inherently unstable, leading to tyranny. Aristotle, in the *Politics*, considered it a "deviant" constitution (rule by the poor for their own interest).

The "Old Oligarch" (Pseudo-Xenophon) sarcastically acknowledged democracy's internal logic while deploring its results.

### Modern Debates

Scholars debate whether Athenian democracy was:
- A remarkable achievement of popular participation
- Limited by its exclusions and imperial context
- A useful model or cautionary tale for modern democracies

## End of Democracy

- **404 BCE**: Sparta imposes the Thirty Tyrants after Peloponnesian War defeat
- **403 BCE**: Democracy restored
- **322 BCE**: Macedonian power ends Athenian independence and democracy

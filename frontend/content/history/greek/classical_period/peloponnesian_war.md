# The Peloponnesian War: Greece's Great Civil Conflict

## Overview

The Peloponnesian War (431-404 BCE) pitted Athens and its maritime empire against Sparta and the Peloponnesian League in a devastating conflict that consumed the Greek world. Lasting 27 years with only brief interruptions, the war ended Athenian predominance, weakened all participants, and ultimately paved the way for Macedonian conquest. Our primary source, Thucydides's *History*, remains one of history's greatest works of political and military analysis.

## Causes of the War

### Structural Causes

Thucydides identified the fundamental cause:
> "The truest cause (*alethestaten prophasin*), though least openly expressed, I believe to have been the growth of Athenian power, which put fear into the Spartans and compelled them to war."

This insight—that a rising power threatening an established one creates structural pressure toward conflict—is now called the "Thucydides Trap."

### The Growth of Athenian Power

After the Persian Wars, Athens transformed the Delian League from an alliance into an empire:
- Allied contributions became tribute payments
- The treasury moved from Delos to Athens (454 BCE)
- Allies who tried to secede were crushed
- Athenian colonies (*cleruchies*) planted on allied territory

### Immediate Pretexts

Three incidents provided pretexts:

1. **Corcyra (433 BCE)**: Athens allied with Corcyra against Corinth
2. **Potidaea (432 BCE)**: Athens besieged this Corinthian colony
3. **Megarian Decree**: Athens banned Megarian trade—economically devastating

Sparta's allies demanded war. After debate, Sparta agreed: Athens must be stopped.

## Phase One: The Archidamian War (431-421 BCE)

### Strategies

**Spartan Strategy**: Annual invasions of Attica to destroy crops and force Athens to fight a land battle.

**Athenian Strategy** (Pericles): Avoid land battle; rely on walls connecting Athens to Piraeus; use naval superiority to raid Peloponnesian coasts; outlast Sparta financially.

### The Plague (430-426 BCE)

Crowded behind the walls, Athens suffered devastating plague (perhaps typhoid fever). One-quarter to one-third of the population died, including Pericles (429 BCE).

Thucydides, himself a survivor, provides a harrowing clinical description:
> "People died like flies... The bodies of the dying were heaped one on top of another."

### Cleon and Brasidas

After Pericles, more aggressive leaders emerged:
- **Cleon**: Athenian demagogue who captured Spartans at Sphacteria (425 BCE)
- **Brasidas**: Brilliant Spartan general who campaigned in Thrace

Both died at Amphipolis (422 BCE), removing the main obstacles to peace.

### Peace of Nicias (421 BCE)

Named for the Athenian negotiator, this treaty was supposed to last 50 years. It returned to the pre-war status quo. In practice, neither side was satisfied, and the peace proved fragile.

## The Sicilian Expedition (415-413 BCE)

### Origins

Segesta in Sicily requested Athenian help against Syracuse. Alcibiades convinced Athens to send a massive expedition to conquer Sicily.

**The Debate**: Nicias argued against the expedition, ironically causing Athens to send an even larger force. The assembly voted for 100 triremes and 5,000+ hoplites.

### The Mutilation of the Herms

On the eve of departure, statues of Hermes throughout Athens were vandalized. Alcibiades was accused of involvement and of profaning the Eleusinian Mysteries. He fled to Sparta rather than face trial.

### Disaster

The expedition was a catastrophe:
- Initial successes; siege of Syracuse began
- Syracuse received Spartan help; Alcibiades advised Spartans
- Athenian reinforcements under Demosthenes arrived too late
- The entire Athenian force was destroyed in the harbor and during retreat
- Approximately 40,000 Athenians and allies were killed or enslaved

Thucydides's assessment:
> "This was the greatest action of this war, and, in my opinion, the greatest action in Greek history—most glorious to the victors, most disastrous to the vanquished."

## The Ionian War (412-404 BCE)

### Sparta's New Strategy

With Alcibiades's advice, Sparta shifted to naval warfare and sought Persian support. The satrap Tissaphernes provided funds to build a fleet.

### Athenian Resilience

Despite losing Sicily, Athens rebuilt its fleet and continued fighting. The democracy was briefly overthrown (411 BCE) but restored.

### Alcibiades's Return

Alcibiades, having worn out his welcome in Sparta, returned to Athens and won victories at Cyzicus (410 BCE) and elsewhere. But after subordinates lost at Notium (406 BCE), he was again exiled.

### Arginusae and Its Aftermath (406 BCE)

Athens won a great naval victory but failed to rescue shipwrecked sailors due to a storm. The generals were tried and executed—a miscarriage of justice that Socrates alone opposed.

### Aegospotami (405 BCE)

The Spartan admiral Lysander, with Persian gold, rebuilt Sparta's fleet. At Aegospotami in the Hellespont, he caught the Athenian fleet beached and captured most of it. Athens's grain supply from the Black Sea was cut off.

### The Siege and Surrender (405-404 BCE)

Besieged by land and sea, Athens faced starvation. Terms were harsh:
- Long Walls demolished
- Fleet reduced to 12 ships
- Delian League dissolved
- Sparta's allies wanted Athens destroyed; Sparta refused, citing Athens's services against Persia

## Aftermath

### The Thirty Tyrants (404-403 BCE)

Sparta installed an oligarchic junta led by Critias. Their reign of terror killed 1,500 Athenians before democrats under Thrasybulus restored democracy.

### Long-Term Consequences

- Athens never fully recovered its power
- Sparta's hegemony proved brief and harsh
- The Greek poleis were weakened for Macedonian conquest
- Thucydides's work shaped historical and political analysis forever

## Thucydides as Historian

### Method

Thucydides established standards for critical history:
> "As for the speeches made by various people... it has been my practice to make the speakers say what I thought the situation demanded, while adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what was actually said."

### Themes

- Power and its limits
- The gap between words and deeds
- Democracy under pressure
- The role of chance (*tyche*) and necessity (*ananke*)
